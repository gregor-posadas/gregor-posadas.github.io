Dad

Dr. Roger Dela Rosa Posadas · October 17, 1944 to August 20, 2017

Relativity physicist, activist, political prisoner, and builder of Philippine science. This is a professional website, and he is the reason I have a profession.

Why this page

My dad was a physicist and a teacher who believed that science should serve the people, and that a country builds itself with the people who know how to build. He went to prison for acting on that belief, and he spent the rest of his life building the institutions that let Filipino scientists train and work at home. He died in August 2017. Everything I have done since, from enlisting to pay for an engineering degree to starting a PhD on how water reaches Filipino households, traces back to him.

As far as I know, this is the first attempt to bring his whole story together in one place. If you knew him, worked with him, or have documents or photographs, please write to me.

Following in his footsteps, I decided to pursue a career in civil engineering to learn how to shape the built environment for the benefit and well-being of both people and nature.

What I told Boise State News in 2025, when they asked why I do this work.

Two lines back to Einstein

In physics, students are counted by who trained them. Dad's line runs back to Albert Einstein twice.

  1. Albert Einstein recommended Philipp Frank as his successor in Prague, 1912.
  2. Frank supervised Peter G. Bergmann's Ph.D. in Prague, 1936. Bergmann then became Einstein's research assistant at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, 1936 to 1941, and co-wrote papers with him.
  3. Bergmann supervised Ezra T. Newman's Ph.D. at Syracuse, 1956. Newman went on to co-create the Newman–Penrose formalism and to discover the Kerr–Newman black hole.
  4. Newman supervised Roger D. Posadas's Ph.D. at Pittsburgh, 1970.

Before physics

He grew up wanting to be a general. The son of an army officer, he moved from school to school with his father's postings and spent his boyhood on make-believe battlefields, reading about Napoleon rather than magnets and batteries. He planned two years of engineering at UP and then the Philippine Military Academy. Relativity got to him first, and studying it cost him the eyesight the academy required.

Activist and political prisoner

Dad did not think a scientist could be neutral. In his view, refusing to take sides only meant your work would end up serving whoever already held power, so he chose the side of the people and organized other scientists to do the same. The Samahan ng Makabayang Siyentipiko took science out of the laboratory: to the barrios, to the oil-price hearings, and to the barricades of the Diliman Commune.

Martial law made that a crime. He spent most of the 1970s underground, and when the military caught him in January 1976 they tortured him and held him for two years and nine months without ever filing charges, first in a safe house, then at Bicutan and Camp Crame.

The world's physicists would not let him disappear. Letters reached Marcos from Caltech, Oxford, Brussels, Pavia, Bern, Waterloo and Pittsburgh, and from the president of the American Physical Society. Amnesty International took up his case, physics conferences passed resolutions in his name, and Physics Today was still asking its readers to write to Malacañang in 1978. Inside, he kept teaching, guiding a student's thesis through the prison walls. He was released in October 1978 and went straight back to work at the same university.

What he built

When he came back, physics at UP was hollowed out; in 1982 the department lost nearly all of its Ph.D.s to jobs abroad. He rebuilt it by giving people reasons to stay: the country's first doctoral program in physics, a five-year bachelor's with a real thesis, laboratories that could do experimental work, and links with universities in Japan. That department became the National Institute of Physics. Then he did the same for the sciences as a whole, carving the College of Science out of Arts and Sciences in 1982, more than doubling its Ph.D. faculty, and raising the buildings that began the UP Science Complex.

He never stopped fighting in public. He wrote an indictment of the government's science board under Marcos, led petitions against a science secretary, called out a deuterium hoax as a swindle, argued for teaching physics in Filipino, and spent his last decades on technonationalism: the idea that the Philippines could catch up only by building its own technological capabilities, as South Korea and Taiwan had. He kept saying Filipinos were as capable as anyone of mastering science.

Reporters kept calling him a nuclear physicist, and he kept correcting them: relativity. His colleagues remember a perfectionist who seemed aloof but was fair, who loved coffee and singing, and who served drinks at his parties but never drank.

The case, and the Supreme Court

In late 1995, while Dad was away on an official trip to China, the campus's officer-in-charge, Vice-Chancellor Rolando Dayco, appointed him project director of the Technology Management Center, the program Dad had conceived, at ₱30,000 a month and backdated to September. He also named Dad the project's local consultant. About a year later the government's resident auditor flagged the payments. ₱247,500 was disallowed and deducted from Dad's terminal leave pay, and in a separate administrative case UP's Board of Regents settled on a forced resignation with the right to reapply after a year.

Both men were then charged with graft under the Anti-Graft and Corrupt Practices Act and with violating the code of conduct for public officials. On June 28, 2005, the Sandiganbayan convicted them and sentenced each to up to 12 years in prison on the first charge and 5 years on the second, with perpetual disqualification from public office. In July 2013 the Supreme Court's First Division upheld the conviction, over a dissent by Justice Associate Justice of the Supreme Court of the Philippines from 2009 to 2014. Learn more (opens in a new tab).

On November 27, 2013, the same division reversed itself. It granted their motions for reconsideration and vacated the conviction because the State had failed to prove their guilt beyond reasonable doubt. Justice Abad, now writing for the Court:

All indications are that they acted in good faith. They were scientists, not lawyers, hence unfamiliar with Civil Service rules and regulations.

Posadas and Dayco v. Sandiganbayan, G.R. Nos. 168951 and 169000, November 27, 2013

The Court also found no evidence that anyone better qualified had been passed over: “The fact is that he was the best qualified for the work.” The ruling came eighteen years after the appointment. Dad was 69.

Nuclear power and national industrialization

He spent his last years arguing that the Philippines could meet its Paris Agreement commitments and still industrialize only by going nuclear: recommissioning the A nuclear plant built in Bataan under Marcos and finished in 1984, but never switched on. The government shelved it after the 1986 revolution, amid safety and corruption concerns. Learn more (opens in a new tab) and building new reactors, including small modular ones, across Luzon, the Visayas and Mindanao. On September 6, 2016, he sent Energy Secretary Alfonso Cusi a first draft of a national nuclear energy policy. Its vision was a Philippine nuclear industry able to design and build its own reactors, not just buy them.

In a short note on the Bataan plant, he pictured it as the hub of an agro-industrial cluster in Bataan, Subic and Zambales, supplying cheap power, industrial heat and desalinated water, with a dedicated university and research institutes growing around it into a “Nucleopolis, a la Silicon Valley.”
