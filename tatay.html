Dr. Roger D. Posadas · physicist, teacher, builder of institutions

A page for my father. This is a professional website, and he is the reason I have a profession.

Why this page

My father was a physicist and a teacher who believed that science should serve people and that a country builds itself with the people who know how to build. He died in August 2017. Everything I have done since, from enlisting to pay for an engineering degree to starting a PhD on how water reaches Filipino households, traces back to him.

Following in his footsteps, I decided to pursue a career in civil engineering to learn how to shape the built environment for the benefit and well-being of both people and nature.

What I told Boise State News in 2025, when they asked why I do this work.

His line

  1. UP Diliman · 1964B.S. Physics, University of the Philippines.
  2. Pittsburgh · 1965 to 1970Scholarship to the University of Pittsburgh. Ph.D. in relativity physics, 1970. He came home.
  3. UP Physics · 1980 to 1992Chair of the Department of Physics. Started the country's first Ph.D. program in physics and the plan that made the National Institute of Physics a national center of excellence.
  4. College of Science · 1983 to 1993Founding dean. Argued for splitting the old College of Arts and Sciences so that science could stand on its own, then built the college that resulted.
  5. UP Diliman · 1993 to 1996Fifth Chancellor of UP Diliman. Conceived and started the UP Science Complex.
  6. 2017Passed away on August 20, 2017, after more than fifty years on the UP faculty.

What he built

He spent his whole career at the University of the Philippines, and most of what he built there is still standing. As dean he helped establish the Center for Environmental Research, Education and Services, the Archaeological Studies Program, and the Materials Science and Engineering Center. He co-founded the Samahan ng Makabayang Siyentipiko and helped establish the Kapisanan ng mga Siyentipikong Pilipino, which created a Ph.D. consortium program and contributed to what became the Philippine Council for Advanced Science and Technology Research. He later coordinated a Technology Management Center project with Canadian support.

The through-line was simple. He thought the Philippines should train its own scientists at home, keep them, and put them to work on the country's problems. He got his doctorate abroad and then spent fifty years making it possible for others not to have to.

His colleagues also remember that he was as serious about singing as he was about UP.

What I carry

I did not become a physicist. I became an engineer, because I wanted to work on the part of his vision that touches people most directly: the pipes, the plants, and the institutions that decide whether a family's tap runs. The questions I ask in my research, about why systems that look fine on paper fail the people who depend on them, are the same questions he asked about Philippine science. I hope to bring the work home, as he did.

Salamat, Tatay.
