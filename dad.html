Dad

Dr. Roger Dela Rosa Posadas · October 17, 1944 to August 20, 2017

Relativity physicist, activist, political prisoner, and builder of Philippine science. This is a professional website, and he is the reason I have a profession.

Why this page

My dad was a physicist and a teacher who believed that science should serve the people, and that a country builds itself with the people who know how to build. He went to prison for acting on that belief, and he spent the rest of his life building the institutions that let Filipino scientists train and work at home. He died in August 2017. Everything I have done since, from enlisting to pay for an engineering degree to starting a PhD on how water reaches Filipino households, traces back to him.

As far as I know, this is the first attempt to bring his whole story together in one place. If you knew him, worked with him, or have documents or photographs, please write to me.

Following in his footsteps, I decided to pursue a career in civil engineering to learn how to shape the built environment for the benefit and well-being of both people and nature.

What I told Boise State News in 2025, when they asked why I do this work.

His line

  1. Lingayen, Pangasinan · 1944Born October 17, 1944, under the Japanese occupation, the eldest of seven children of Col. Rolando Posadas of the Philippine Army and Carmen de la Rosa. Our family's story is that he was born in hiding in the jungle during the war, and that his father later had to swear an affidavit before he could be registered, once civil offices reopened.
  2. Cagayan de Oro · 1960Changed schools wherever his father was posted. Graduated valedictorian of Xavier University High School, set on following his father into the Philippine Military Academy. His eyesight weakened from studying, and he never went.
  3. UP Diliman · 1960 to 1964Entered the University of the Philippines on a National Science Development Board scholarship, writing simply "Physics" on the form. Einstein's relativity took hold of him in his third year. Founding president of the UP Physics Society. B.S. Physics, 1964, then a year as an instructor.
  4. Pittsburgh · 1965 to 1970A Rockefeller Foundation grant took him to the University of Pittsburgh and its relativity group under Ezra T. Newman. He skipped the master's degree and finished the Ph.D. in May 1970, in four and a half years, with a dissertation on the motion and structure of singularities in general relativity. He published in Physical Review Letters, Physical Review and the Journal of Mathematical Physics, visited the relativity groups in London and Cambridge, and kept the scholarship's promise to come home.
  5. UP Physics · 1970Assistant professor in June 1970 and chair of the physics department a month later, at 25. He founded the Samahan ng Makabayang Siyentipiko (SMS), the country's first organization of nationalist scientists and science students, and co-founded the activist teachers' union SAGUPA.
  6. Diliman Commune · 1971When students and faculty barricaded the campus in February 1971, SMS scientists made the self-igniting molotovs its defenders used against the Metrocom, and SMS produced the first serious study of the 1970 oil price hikes. After the Plaza Miranda bombing and the suspension of habeas corpus that August, his name was on a list of activists wanted for subversion. He went underground, and stayed there through martial law.
  7. Arrested · January 1976Captured in January 1976 and branded by the regime as head of the Communist Party's military research. He was tortured during interrogation, held incommunicado for more than two months, and then detained at Bicutan and Camp Crame. He was never formally charged.
  8. The campaign · 1976 to 1978Amnesty International named him a prisoner of conscience. Kip Thorne, Roger Penrose and Dennis Sciama, Werner Israel, Bruno Bertotti, Heinrich Leutwyler, his Pittsburgh teachers Allen Janis and Ezra Newman, and the president of the American Physical Society wrote to Marcos. The Texas Symposium on Relativistic Astrophysics passed a resolution regretting his absence. From prison he supervised a UP student's master's thesis by passing calculations back and forth.
  9. Released · October 1978Freed after two years and nine months. That same year he was invited onto the organizing committee of the Einstein Centennial, the only physicist from Southeast Asia.
  10. UP · 1980 to 1993Chair of physics again from January 1980. He lengthened the B.S. to five years with a required thesis and started the country's first Ph.D. program in physics. In 1981 he helped found the Kilusan ng mga Siyentipikong Pilipino. As associate dean he turned physics, geology and the Natural Sciences Research Center into national centers of excellence, then split the sciences off into a new College of Science in 1983 and led it as founding dean for three terms. Its Ph.D. faculty grew from 44 to 104, and its new buildings became the start of the UP Science Complex. He was named an Outstanding Young Scientist in 1984 and stood with the UP physicists in the crowds at EDSA in February 1986.
  11. Chancellor · 1993 to 1996Appointed Chancellor of UP Diliman on November 1, 1993, and installed on July 22, 1994, under the banner "Nationalistic Culture, Futuristic Technology." He pushed Filipino as a medium of instruction, founded the Technology Management Center in 1995, and set up the UP Diliman Information Office, the Center for Environmental Research, Education and Services, the Archaeological Studies Program and the Materials Science and Engineering Center. He also stood up for students when NBI agents tried to make arrests on campus without warrants.
  12. Technology management · 1996 to 2016Turned to how countries catch up: technology management, industrial policy, and what the Philippines could learn from South Korea and Taiwan. He taught in the Technology Management Center, served in science and technology roles at STI and the University of the East, and led the Research and Innovation Center of Lyceum of the Philippines University in Cavite. At 69 he was nominated for UP Diliman chancellor again.
  13. 2017Died on August 20, 2017, at 72.

Two lines back to Einstein

In physics, students are counted by who trained them. Dad's line runs back to Albert Einstein twice.

  1. Albert Einstein recommended Philipp Frank as his successor in Prague, 1912.
  2. Frank supervised Peter G. Bergmann's Ph.D. in Prague, 1936. Bergmann then became Einstein's research assistant at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, 1936 to 1941, and co-wrote papers with him.
  3. Bergmann supervised Ezra T. Newman's Ph.D. at Syracuse, 1956. Newman went on to co-create the Newman–Penrose formalism and to discover the Kerr–Newman black hole.
  4. Newman supervised Roger D. Posadas's Ph.D. at Pittsburgh, 1970.

Newman's collaborator Roger Penrose was one of the physicists who wrote to Marcos for Dad's release. Sources: Mathematics Genealogy Project, INSPIRE-HEP, the Institute for Advanced Study, and Physics Today.

Before physics

He grew up wanting to be a general. The son of an army officer, he moved from school to school with his father's postings and spent his boyhood on make-believe battlefields, reading about Napoleon rather than magnets and batteries. He planned two years of engineering at UP and then the Philippine Military Academy. Relativity got to him first, and studying it cost him the eyesight the academy required.

Activist and political prisoner

Dad did not think a scientist could be neutral. In his view, refusing to take sides only meant your work would end up serving whoever already held power, so he chose the side of the people and organized other scientists to do the same. The Samahan ng Makabayang Siyentipiko took science out of the laboratory: to the barrios, to the oil-price hearings, and to the barricades of the Diliman Commune.

Martial law made that a crime. He spent most of the 1970s underground, and when the military caught him in January 1976 they tortured him and held him for two years and nine months without ever filing charges, first in a safe house, then at Bicutan and Camp Crame.

The world's physicists would not let him disappear. Letters reached Marcos from Caltech, Oxford, Brussels, Pavia, Bern, Waterloo and Pittsburgh, and from the president of the American Physical Society. Amnesty International adopted him as a prisoner of conscience, physics conferences passed resolutions in his name, and Physics Today was still asking its readers to write to Malacañang in 1978. Inside, he kept teaching, guiding a student's thesis through the prison walls. He was released in October 1978 and went straight back to work at the same university.

What he built

When he came back, physics at UP was hollowed out; in 1982 the department lost nearly all of its Ph.D.s to jobs abroad. He rebuilt it by giving people reasons to stay: the country's first doctoral program in physics, a five-year bachelor's with a real thesis, laboratories that could do experimental work, and links with universities in Japan. That department became the National Institute of Physics. Then he did the same for the sciences as a whole, carving the College of Science out of Arts and Sciences in 1983, more than doubling its Ph.D. faculty, and raising the buildings that began the UP Science Complex.

He never stopped fighting in public. He wrote an indictment of the government's science board under Marcos, led petitions against a science secretary, called out a deuterium hoax as a swindle, argued for teaching physics in Filipino, and spent his last decades on technonationalism: the idea that the Philippines could catch up only by building its own technological capabilities, as South Korea and Taiwan had. He kept saying Filipinos were as capable as anyone of mastering science.

Reporters kept calling him a nuclear physicist, and he kept correcting them: relativity. His colleagues remember a perfectionist who seemed aloof but was fair, who loved coffee and singing, and who served drinks at his parties but never drank.

The chancellor's book

When he was installed as Chancellor of UP Diliman on July 22, 1994, the university gave him a commemorative book: Pagtatalaga kay Dr. Roger Posadas bilang Ika-limang Chanselor ng Unibersidad ng Pilipinas Diliman. It holds his inaugural address in English and Filipino, a biography by Dean Rogelio Sicat, and messages from colleagues, students and universities around the world. It is the source for much of the timeline above, including the date of his release.

Ester Garcia, arrested in the same sweep in 1976 and by then a commissioner of higher education, wrote that he sacrificed his own research career to build physics in the country. Danilo Yanga, whose master's thesis Dad supervised from prison, became dean of the college Dad founded. The student regent introduced him to students as the Diliman Commune activist famous for his molotovs.

Dad and me

What I carry

I did not become a physicist. I became an engineer, because I wanted to work on the part of his vision that touches people most directly: the pipes, the plants, and the institutions that decide whether a family's tap runs. The questions I ask in my research, about why systems that look fine on paper fail the people who depend on them, are the same questions he asked about Philippine science. I hope to bring the work home, as he did.

Thank you, Dad.
