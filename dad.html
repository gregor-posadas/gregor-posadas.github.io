Dad

Dr. Roger Dela Rosa Posadas · October 17, 1944 to August 20, 2017

Relativity physicist, activist, political prisoner, and builder of Philippine science. This is a professional website, and he is the reason I have a profession.

Why this page

My dad was a physicist and a teacher who believed that science should serve the people, and that a country builds itself with the people who know how to build. He went to prison for acting on that belief, and he spent the rest of his life building the institutions that let Filipino scientists train and work at home. He died in August 2017. Everything I have done since, from enlisting to pay for an engineering degree to starting a PhD on how water reaches Filipino households, traces back to him.

As far as I know, this is the first attempt to bring his whole story together in one place. If you knew him, worked with him, or have documents or photographs, please write to me.

Before physics

He grew up wanting to be a general. The son of an army officer, he moved from school to school with his father's postings and spent his boyhood on make-believe battlefields, reading about Napoleon rather than magnets and batteries. He planned two years of engineering at UP and then the Philippine Military Academy. Relativity got to him first, and studying it cost him the eyesight the academy required.

His dissertation, 1970

Roger's doctoral thesis took on one of the oldest problems in Einstein's theory of gravity (1915), in which mass and energy curve spacetime and that curvature tells matter how to move. Learn more (opens in a new tab): how Einstein's equations themselves dictate the way a body moves. In Isaac Newton's physics (1687). His law of gravity says how masses pull on each other; his separate laws of motion say how things move when a force acts on them. Learn more (opens in a new tab) you need one law for how masses make gravity and a second law for how things move. In Einstein's theory the first law already contains the second, but turning that into usable equations is hard.

Think of a boat on a still lake. Even without seeing the shore, you could tell how it moved from the ripples it leaves: they crowd together ahead of it and spread apart behind. Activist and political prisoner

Roger did not think a scientist could be neutral. In his view, refusing to take sides only meant your work would end up serving whoever already held power, so he chose the side of the people and organized other scientists to do the same. The Samahan ng Makabayang Siyentipiko took science out of the laboratory: to the barrios, to the oil-price hearings, and to the barricades of the Diliman Commune.

Martial law made that a crime. He spent most of the 1970s underground, and when the military caught him in January 1976 they tortured him and held him for two years and nine months without ever filing charges, first in a safe house, then at Bicutan and Camp Crame.

The world's physicists would not let him disappear. Letters reached Marcos from Caltech, Oxford, Brussels, Pavia, Bern, Waterloo and Pittsburgh, and from the president of the American Physical Society. Amnesty International took up his case, physics conferences passed resolutions in his name, and The monthly magazine of the American Institute of Physics, published since 1948. What he built

When he came back, physics at UP was hollowed out; in 1982 the department lost nearly all of its Ph.D.s to jobs abroad. He rebuilt it by giving people reasons to stay: the country's first doctoral program in physics, a five-year bachelor's with a real thesis, laboratories that could do experimental work, and links with universities in Japan. That department became the National Institute of Physics. Then he did the same for the sciences as a whole, carving the College of Science out of Arts and Sciences in 1982, more than doubling its Ph.D. faculty, and raising the buildings that began the UP Science Complex.

If I had the power, I would lay a simple stone in his name in one remote corner of that magnificent Complex in recognition of the power of his vision.

A UP professor emeritus of sociology, he was founding director of UP's Third World Studies Center under the Marcos dictatorship, hosted the public-affairs TV show Public Forum from 1986, and has written the Inquirer column “Public Lives” since 1995. He won Japan's Fukuoka Prize in 2019. Learn more (opens in a new tab), at Roger's necrological service, August 24, 2017 (UP Diliman (opens in a new tab))

He never stopped fighting in public. He wrote an indictment of the government's science board under Marcos, led petitions against a science secretary, called out a deuterium hoax as a swindle, argued for teaching physics in Filipino, and spent his last decades on technonationalism: the idea that the Philippines could catch up only by building its own technological capabilities, as South Korea and Taiwan had. He kept saying Filipinos were as capable as anyone of mastering science.

He could be blunt about national pride. Speaking to Philippine Navy officers in 2012, he took aim at a national icon:

Another glaring example of Philippine technological weakness is the The Philippines' most common public transport: long, brightly decorated minibuses first built from surplus US Army jeeps after World War II. Learn more (opens in a new tab), which some Filipinos like to claim as the embodiment of Filipino ingenuity but which to me exemplifies Filipino inability to achieve self-reliance in designing and manufacturing an internal combustion engine – a hundred-year old technology that Koreans, Malaysians, and Chinese have learned to design and produce on their own.

Roger Posadas, speech to the Philippine Navy Governance Forum, November 9, 2012 (reproduced online (opens in a new tab))

Reporters kept calling him a nuclear physicist, and he kept correcting them: relativity. His colleagues remember a perfectionist who seemed aloof but was fair, who loved coffee and singing, and who served drinks at his parties but never drank.

Believe it or not, Roger was as serious about singing as he was serious about UP.

His inaugural address

He framed his term around two milestones: the centennial of the The war for independence from Spain, launched in August 1896 by the secret society Katipunan. Its centennial fell in 1996. Learn more (opens in a new tab) that gave birth to the Filipino nation, and the year 2000, which he saw, after Alvin Toffler, as the gateway to a knowledge-based “Third Wave” civilization. Measured against both, he said, the country had fallen behind:

Our first 100 years as a nation have been a century of cultural deterioration and technological stagnation.

Inaugural address, July 22, 1994

The Philippines, he pointed out, had “not even mastered some of the most basic technologies of the 19th century such as steel making, chemical production and machine tool making,” while its East Asian neighbors industrialized by mastering Western technology and keeping their own cultures. His answer was the slogan of his term, “Nationalistic Culture, Futuristic Technology”: UP Diliman should become the spearhead of both.

In practice that meant reviving general education as the heart of undergraduate study, adding a course on ethics and social responsibility, shifting undergraduate teaching from English to Filipino while strengthening the English department, building a 30-hectare Arts and Culture Complex, and drafting “U.P. Diliman 2008,” a plan running to the university's centennial. By October 1996 he promised a computerized, networked campus, modernized laboratories and libraries, and the start of a Technology Park and an Engineering Complex.

He closed with appeals. To the faculty, “underpaid and overworked” and expected to produce world-class work “with third-class facilities,” he asked for a culture of research and creativity. To the students, he asked them to “breathe life to the activism that once gave this campus the distinction of being the vanguard of the nationalistic commitment and idealism” UP was known for.

Catching up: industrialization and technonationalism

For the last three decades of his life, Roger kept returning to one question. The Philippines had started to industrialize before most of its neighbors. How did it end up behind nearly all of them, and what would it take to catch up? He worked on it in papers from the 1980s to 2015, in a 2012 lecture to the The Philippine Navy invited him in 2012 to speak in its Governance Forum series for officers., in four Saturday-evening talks to the Diliman Book Club in 2014 on “Philippine S&T Backwardness and National Technology Catch-up,” and in his last proposals on energy. He gave his answer a name, The view that a nation's security and prosperity depend on building and controlling its own technology rather than relying on foreign suppliers. The ladder

To explain the gap, he looked past what a country's firms owned to what they could actually do with a technology. He pictured it as a ladder. On the lowest rung a firm can only operate a machine it bought. Higher up it can adapt the machine to local conditions, rebuild the whole plant without outside help, copy it by Taking a product apart to learn how it was made, then making your own version. Learn more (opens in a new tab), improve on it, and finally invent something new from its own research. He refined the ladder over the years, from five rungs in 1989 to nine in his later papers.

A firm at the lowest or operative rung of the technological ladder is a mere technology user, while one at the highest or creative rung is a technology creator, pioneer, and leader.

By his assessment, almost all Filipino-owned firms had never climbed past the bottom two rungs. “In general,” he wrote, “Philippine domestic firms do not make any effort to reverse-engineer or improve an imported technology; they simply buy another set of foreign equipment or license another foreign process when they decide to replace their technology.” His favorite example was electricity. The The National Power Corporation, the government company that built and ran most of the Philippines' power plants from 1936 until the power sector was privatized from 2001. A vicious circle

Why didn't the firms climb? He traced it to a loop. Because the country imported its technology, there was almost no demand for Filipino science and engineering. With no demand, neither government nor industry saw a reason to invest in research and training. With little investment, local scientists and engineers could not meet industry's needs, so the country imported even more. He first described the loop in the 1980s and returned to it in nearly every paper after. “This vicious circle,” he told the Navy in 2012, “is the basic central problem of the Philippines.”

He saw historical roots as well. In his 1994 inaugural address as chancellor of UP Diliman he put it this way:

Since the start of the 20th century, our elite has been propagating American culture while neglecting the mastery of western technology. In contrast, Japan and other East Asian countries were able to industrialize and catch up with the advanced countries by mastering western technology while preserving their national culture.

Inaugural address, July 22, 1994

He named the two choices a country faces. The second was technonationalism. In his 2012 definition, it holds that “long-term strategic national interests should take precedence over short-term comparative advantages on matters involving technology selection, acquisition, and exploitation,” that self-reliance in strategic technologies is a matter of national security, and that building the country's science and technology to world-class levels is a national imperative.

Technonationalism did not mean shutting foreign technology out. Japan, South Korea and Taiwan had imported theirs, through licenses, machinery, joint ventures and reverse engineering. The difference, he argued, was that their firms set out to master what they bought. These late industrializers, he wrote, “deliberately defied the principles of comparative advantage” to build steel mills, shipyards, car and chip factories, machine tools and power plants. Leapfrogging

Catching up rung by rung was not his only idea. In 1989, he and Celso Roque argued that a latecomer could skip stages. Using Alvin Toffler's Alvin Toffler's division of history into three waves: farming and crafts, then the industrial age, then the information age. Roger and Roque used it to sort technologies by age. Learn more (opens in a new tab) of technology, they wrote that “the Philippines has no choice but to leapfrog the First Wave and Second Wave Technologies and master selected, strategic areas of Third Wave Technologies.” Their example: rather than build up the old, steel-based machine tool industry, master industrial lasers for cutting, drilling and welding.

The basic idea is to master, whenever feasible, the state-of-the-art technology rather than to invest money and efforts in acquiring competence in the corresponding obsolete technology.

Posadas and Roque, “Toward a leapfrogging strategy for national self-reliance in science and technology,” Asian Studies, 1989

They also argued that comparative advantage “is not absolute and permanent but rather subject to technology”: synthetic materials and automation were already eroding the edge of countries that lived on raw materials and cheap labor. And they warned that nations without technological mastery could become “technological colonies” in the 21st century.

He applied the same thinking to energy. In a 2015 op-ed with Australian scholar of strategy at Macquarie University in Sydney, who studies how East Asian countries caught up in high-tech industries, including renewable energy. His papers end with long lists of recommendations. The main ones:

  • A national technology strategy and roadmap aimed at catching up, with regular technology audits that measure how far each industry lags the world's best.
  • Industrial A group of related companies, suppliers and research centers in one place that feed each other's work, like Silicon Valley. Learn more (opens in a new tab) in every district and province, linking big firms with small ones and foreign companies with Filipino ones, each with its own training and research centers.
  • Research spending raised to the United Nations target of 1% of GDP, world-class research universities, and a “massive crash program” to train more scientists and engineers.
  • Government research institutes given corporate powers like Taiwan's Taiwan's Industrial Technology Research Institute, a government research body founded in 1973 that spun off companies such as the chipmaker TSMC. Learn more (opens in a new tab), and a contract research organization like Korea's The Korea Institute of Science and Technology, founded in 1966, South Korea's first government research institute for industry. Learn more (opens in a new tab) or Germany's Germany's network of applied research institutes, which do research under contract for companies. Learn more (opens in a new tab).

  • Foreign investment used to seed clusters, not relied on to industrialize.
  • In each firm, a chief technology officer, and the goal of moving from making products to a foreign company's design (Original equipment manufacturing: making products to a foreign company's exact design, to be sold under its brand. The next steps are own design (ODM) and own brand (OBM). Learn more (opens in a new tab)) to its own designs and then its own brand, as Samsung and Acer had.
  • A four-year Bachelor of Technology, “reinventing technicians as ‘Technologists,’” and technology management courses across business, engineering and government.

Our country's industrial development program should not depend mainly on FDIs which view the country as a mere temporary investment base within their global strategy.

“Technology management and catch-up competitiveness,” 2006

He was not starry-eyed about the countries he held up as models. South Korea's government targeting worked in electronics, steel and shipbuilding but failed in chemicals and machinery, he noted, and its South Korea's giant family-run business groups, such as Samsung and Hyundai. Learn more (opens in a new tab) neglected small suppliers, leaving them dependent on Japanese parts. Taiwan, built on small and medium firms, offered a different route. And state-led industrialization, he warned, had a precondition:

State intervention in the economy can be effective for only as long as the government's leaders and technocrats are competent and incorruptible.

“Technology management and catch-up competitiveness,” 2006

His own summary, from his 2015 paper on export competitiveness: “a country or a firm can become an export tiger only if it is also a technology tiger.” Philippine firms, he concluded, “have not yet developed into export tigers up to now because they have remained technological pussycats.”

Nuclear power and national industrialization

He spent his last years arguing that the Philippines could meet its Paris Agreement commitments and still industrialize only by going nuclear: recommissioning the A nuclear plant built in Bataan under Marcos and finished in 1984, but never switched on. The government shelved it after the 1986 revolution, amid safety and corruption concerns. Learn more (opens in a new tab) and building new reactors, including Nuclear reactors rated below 300 megawatts, designed to be built in factories as modules and assembled on site. Learn more (opens in a new tab), across Luzon, the Visayas and Mindanao. On September 6, 2016, he sent Energy Secretary Secretary of Energy under President Rodrigo Duterte, from 2016 to 2022. Learn more (opens in a new tab)Photo: IAEA Imagebank, 2022 (opens in a new tab) · CC BY 2.0 (cropped) a first draft of a national nuclear energy policy. Its vision was a Philippine nuclear industry able to design and build its own reactors, not just buy them.

Before he turned to nuclear power, he made the same argument for solar and wind. In December 2015 he and the Australian scholar John A. Mathews wrote in the Philippine Daily Inquirer:

In a country with such abundance of renewable energy from the sun, the wind and the earth, it is madness to keep importing fossil fuels like coal, oil and gas, and to delay the inevitable shift to renewables that is already under way.

John A. Mathews and Roger D. Posadas, “How to achieve energy security and restart industrialization” (opens in a new tab), December 13, 2015

What mattered most to him was who would build the technology. Importing foreign-made turbines and panels, they warned, would only trade one dependence for another: imported fuel for imported equipment.

In a short note on the Bataan plant, he pictured it as the hub of an agro-industrial cluster in Bataan, Subic and Zambales, supplying cheap power, industrial heat and desalinated water, with a dedicated university and research institutes growing around it into a “Nucleopolis, a la Silicon Valley.”
