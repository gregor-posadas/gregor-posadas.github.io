Dr. Roger D. Posadas · physicist, teacher, builder of institutions

A page for my father. This is a professional website, and he is the reason I have a profession.

Why this page

My father was a physicist and a teacher who believed that science should serve people and that a country builds itself with the people who know how to build. He was imprisoned for that belief, and he spent the rest of his life building the institutions that let Filipino scientists train and work at home. He died in August 2017. Everything I have done since, from enlisting to pay for an engineering degree to starting a PhD on how water reaches Filipino households, traces back to him.

Following in his footsteps, I decided to pursue a career in civil engineering to learn how to shape the built environment for the benefit and well-being of both people and nature.

What I told Boise State News in 2025, when they asked why I do this work.

His line

  1. Cagayan de Oro · 1960Graduated valedictorian of Xavier University High School. The son of an Army officer, he planned to follow his father into the Philippine Military Academy.
  2. UP Diliman · 1960 to 1964Went to the University of the Philippines on a National Science Development Board scholarship. Einstein's theory of relativity, which he met in his third year, changed his plans for good. B.S. Physics, 1964.
  3. Pittsburgh · 1965 to 1970A Rockefeller Foundation scholar at the University of Pittsburgh, chosen for its strong relativity group. Ph.D., 1970, with a dissertation on the equations of motion and the structure of singularities in general relativity, published in Physical Review. Then he came home.
  4. UP Physics · 1970Head of the physics department at 26. That September he and nine other scientists founded the Samahan ng Makabayang Siyentipiko, a society of nationalist scientists, with him as national chairman.
  5. 1971 to 1976Targeted for arrest for his political views, he went underground in 1971. He was picked up in January 1976 and held without formal charges.
  6. 1976 to 1978Physicists around the world, among them Kip Thorne, Roger Penrose, Dennis Sciama, Werner Israel and his Pittsburgh teachers, wrote to President Marcos asking for his release. Amnesty International took up his case. In August 1978 Physics Today was still listing him among imprisoned scientists.
  7. UP · 1980sBack at the university as chair of physics, then associate dean, then founding dean of the College of Science. Named an Outstanding Young Scientist by the National Academy of Science and Technology in 1984. On February 24, 1986, the UP physicists joined the crowds of the People Power Revolution.
  8. UP Diliman · 1993 to 1996Fifth Chancellor of UP Diliman. Conceived and started the UP Science Complex.
  9. Technology management · 1990s to 2010sTurned to how countries catch up: technology management, industrial policy, and what the Philippines could learn from South Korea and Taiwan. He kept writing into his seventies, later from the Research and Innovation Center of Lyceum of the Philippines University in Cavite.
  10. 2017Passed away on August 20, 2017, after more than fifty years on the UP faculty.

Before physics

He grew up wanting to be a general. As a boy he read about Napoleon's campaigns rather than about magnets and batteries, and he expected to follow his father, an Army colonel, into the military. Relativity got to him first.

Held for his convictions

He believed a scientist could stay objective without standing apart from a society in upheaval, and that science should be brought out of the laboratory to the people it was meant to serve. Under martial law that belief was treated as a crime. Targeted for arrest in 1971, he went underground. In January 1976 he was picked up and detained at Bicutan without formal charges. That August the government presented him to the press alongside other detainees as a leader of the communist movement.

The scientific world answered. Letters reached President Marcos from Oxford, Brussels, Pavia, Bern, Caltech, Waterloo and Pittsburgh, some describing him as the only specialist in Einstein's general relativity in the country and asking that his talents be returned to it. Church groups and the scientists who wrote on his behalf reported that he had been tortured in custody. Amnesty International groups in New York and Australia took up his case, and two years after his arrest Physics Today was still asking its readers to write to Malacañang on his behalf. His jailing is still cited, in Richard Pierre Claude's Science in the Service of Human Rights, as an example of scientists persecuted for criticizing a dictatorship.

He came out and went back to work at the same university.

What he built

He spent his whole career at the University of the Philippines, and most of what he built there is still standing. In 1982 the physics department lost nearly all of its Ph.D.s to jobs and grants abroad. He rebuilt it by giving students reasons to stay: a doctoral program at home, advanced courses in lasers and semiconductors, and laboratories that could do real experimental work. That department became the National Institute of Physics, a national center of excellence.

As dean he helped establish the Center for Environmental Research, Education and Services, the Archaeological Studies Program, and the Materials Science and Engineering Center. He helped found the KSP, a national organization of practicing scientists, in 1981 and was elected its first vice president. He later coordinated a Technology Management Center project with Canadian support. Throughout, he wrote publicly and bluntly about how the government funded science, and he never stopped arguing that Filipinos were as capable as anyone of mastering it.

The through-line was simple. He thought the Philippines should train its own scientists at home, keep them, and put them to work on the country's problems. He got his doctorate abroad and then spent fifty years making it possible for others not to have to.

He spent years correcting reporters who called him a nuclear physicist; his field was relativity. His colleagues also remember that he was as serious about singing as he was about UP.

Tatay and me

What I carry

I did not become a physicist. I became an engineer, because I wanted to work on the part of his vision that touches people most directly: the pipes, the plants, and the institutions that decide whether a family's tap runs. The questions I ask in my research, about why systems that look fine on paper fail the people who depend on them, are the same questions he asked about Philippine science. I hope to bring the work home, as he did.

Salamat, Tatay.
